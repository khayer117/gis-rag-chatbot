# Bangladesh Water Resources

## 1. Overview of Water Resources
Bangladesh is one of the most water-rich countries in the world, yet paradoxically faces
severe water stress. The country receives abundant rainfall from the monsoon and is crossed
by over 700 rivers. However, seasonal variability, transboundary dependency, flood and
drought extremes, and water quality issues make water resource management a critical challenge.

## 2. Transboundary River Dependency
About 93% of Bangladesh's surface water originates outside its borders, flowing in from
India and China through the Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna (GBM) basin. This makes Bangladesh
highly dependent on upstream water management decisions. Transboundary river flow data is
critical for flood forecasting and water allocation planning.

## 3. The Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna (GBM) Basin
The GBM basin is one of the world's largest river basins, draining about 1.75 million km²
across Bangladesh, India, Nepal, Bhutan, and China. The combined discharge at the delta
mouth is among the highest in the world. Bangladesh occupies the lowest riparian position,
receiving all upstream flows before they drain into the Bay of Bengal.

## 4. Padma River
The Padma is the main distributary of the Ganges in Bangladesh, entering from India at
Rajshahi. It is one of the widest rivers in Bangladesh during monsoon. The Padma Multipurpose
Bridge (opened 2022) crosses it near Mawa. Flow is regulated upstream by the Farakka Barrage
in India, which significantly reduces dry-season discharge affecting irrigation in northwest Bangladesh.

## 5. Jamuna River
The Jamuna is the local name for the lower Brahmaputra in Bangladesh. It is one of the
most braided and dynamic rivers in the world, with a channel width exceeding 15 km in places.
It carries enormous sediment loads that create and destroy char islands continuously.
The Bangabandhu Bridge crosses the Jamuna connecting the east and west of the country.

## 6. Meghna River
The Meghna forms near Chandpur where the Padma and Jamuna merge, and flows south into the
Bay of Bengal. The lower Meghna estuary is extremely wide (up to 100 km) and is the primary
drainage outlet for the entire GBM basin. Tidal influence extends far upstream in the Meghna.

## 7. Surma–Kushiyara River System
The Surma and Kushiyara rivers in Sylhet division are distributaries of the Barak River
entering from India. They merge to form the upper Meghna. The Surma–Kushiyara system
drains the Haor basin and carries significant flash flood risk during pre-monsoon heavy
rainfall events in the upstream hills of Assam and Meghalaya.

## 8. Brahmaputra Sedimentation
The Brahmaputra (Jamuna) carries one of the highest sediment loads of any river globally,
estimated at 500–700 million tonnes per year. This sediment builds Bangladesh's delta
and creates dynamic morphological changes. Remote sensing and EPSG:32646 projected
imagery are used to monitor channel migration and char formation annually.

## 9. Delta Formation and Land Accretion
The Bengal Delta is still actively forming. Sediment deposition in the coastal zone
causes land accretion (new land formation) of several square kilometers per year in
some areas. Bangladesh's Chars Development and Settlement Project monitors newly accreted
chars and allocates them to landless communities. Accretion monitoring uses multi-temporal
Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery in EPSG:32646.

## 10. Floodplain Area
Approximately 80% of Bangladesh is floodplain. The country is divided into 30 hydrological
units by the Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB). These units guide flood management,
irrigation planning, and drainage improvement. GIS layers of floodplain extent are maintained
in EPSG:32646 by BWDB.

## 11. Annual Flood Cycle
Flooding in Bangladesh follows a predictable annual cycle:
- March–May: pre-monsoon flash floods in Haor areas (borna floods)
- June–September: monsoon riverine and rainfall flooding
- October–November: recession floods and waterlogging
Normal annual flooding affects 20–30% of land area; severe years exceed 60%.

## 12. 1998 Mega Flood
The 1998 flood was the most severe in recorded history for Bangladesh, inundating about
68% of the country for nearly three months. An estimated 30 million people were displaced.
The flood was caused by simultaneous peak flows in the Padma, Jamuna, and Meghna combined
with exceptionally high monsoon rainfall. It remains the benchmark event for flood modeling.

## 13. Flood Forecasting and Warning System
The Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre (FFWC) under BWDB operates a network of river
gauging stations and issues daily flood bulletins. The system uses upstream gauge data,
satellite rainfall estimates (TRMM, GPM), and hydrological models. Forecast lead time is
typically 24–72 hours. GIS-based inundation mapping is disseminated via web portals.

## 14. Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB)
BWDB is the primary agency responsible for water resource development and flood management.
It operates over 8,000 km of embankments, 5,000 km of drainage canals, and hundreds of
water control structures (sluice gates, regulators). BWDB maintains a spatial database
of all hydraulic infrastructure in EPSG:32646.

## 15. Flood Embankment Network
Bangladesh has an extensive network of flood embankments (polders in coastal areas, river
embankments inland) totaling over 8,000 km. These protect agricultural land and settlements
but also trap drainage, causing waterlogging inside protected areas after heavy rain.
Embankment locations and conditions are tracked in GIS by BWDB.

## 16. Coastal Polders
The coastal zone of Bangladesh contains 139 polders — low-lying areas enclosed by earthen
embankments with tidal sluice gates to prevent saltwater intrusion and tidal flooding.
Built in the 1960s–1970s, many polders are now degraded. The Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100
includes rehabilitation of polders with tidal river management (TRM) approaches.

## 17. Tidal River Management (TRM)
TRM is a nature-based approach where controlled breaches are made in polder embankments
to allow tidal flow to deposit sediment inside the polder, raising land level and restoring
drainage capacity. It has been successfully applied in the southwest coastal zone. TRM
effectiveness is monitored using DEM analysis in EPSG:32646.

## 18. Groundwater Resources
Bangladesh has extensive groundwater resources in alluvial aquifers. The main aquifer is
unconfined and lies 5–30 m below ground. Annual groundwater extraction is estimated at
30–35 km³, primarily for irrigation. The northwest (Barind Tract) and north-central areas
are most dependent on groundwater due to limited surface water availability in dry season.

## 19. Groundwater Level Trends
Long-term monitoring shows declining groundwater levels in heavily irrigated areas,
particularly the Barind Tract in Rajshahi and Chapai Nawabganj. Annual decline rates of
0.5–1.0 m/year have been recorded in some areas due to intensive dry-season irrigation
with deep tube wells. BWDB and BADC (Bangladesh Agricultural Development Corporation)
maintain groundwater monitoring well networks.

## 20. Arsenic in Groundwater
Naturally occurring arsenic in shallow tube wells (<100 m depth) affects approximately
20 million people in Bangladesh. The highest arsenic concentrations are found in the
Meghna floodplain districts including Comilla, Chandpur, Munshiganj, and Brahmanbaria.
DPHE maintains GIS maps of arsenic contamination levels by union (lowest admin unit).

## 21. Safe Water Coverage
Access to safe drinking water in Bangladesh has improved significantly. UNICEF and WHO
data indicate over 97% of the population has access to at least basic water services,
but arsenic contamination and dry-season scarcity remain major quality challenges.
Piped water supply in urban areas is managed by city corporations and water utilities.

## 22. Dhaka Water Supply — DWASA
Dhaka Water Supply and Sewerage Authority (DWASA) supplies water to Dhaka city from
two main sources: groundwater (about 78%) and surface water from the Sitalakkhya River
(about 22%). Overreliance on groundwater is causing land subsidence in Dhaka at rates
of 1–3 cm/year. Shifting supply to surface water is a stated policy goal.

## 23. Surface Water Irrigation
The Bangladesh Haor and Flood Control Development Board and BWDB operate large surface
water irrigation projects. The Ganges–Kobadak (G-K) Project in the southwest and the
Teesta Barrage Project in the north are major surface irrigation schemes. Together they
serve millions of hectares of agricultural land.

## 24. Teesta River and Water Sharing
The Teesta river in northwest Bangladesh is critical for dry-season irrigation of the
Rangpur and Rajshahi divisions. A long-pending Teesta Water Sharing Treaty between
Bangladesh and India remains unsigned, causing severe dry-season water shortages
in the Teesta basin when India's Gajoldoba Barrage diverts flow for West Bengal irrigation.

## 25. Farakka Barrage Impact
The Farakka Barrage, built by India in 1975 on the Ganges about 18 km from the Bangladesh
border, diverts Ganges water into the Hooghly River. A bilateral Ganges Water Treaty was
signed in 1996 for 30 years to guarantee minimum dry-season flows to Bangladesh.
However, actual flows often fall below agreed minimums, affecting agriculture and navigation
in the Padma basin and increasing salinity intrusion in the southwest.

## 26. Salinity Intrusion
Reduced freshwater flow into the southwest coastal zone due to the Farakka Barrage and
sea level rise has led to increased salinity intrusion in rivers and groundwater.
The salinity front has moved inland by 100–150 km over the past 50 years in some areas.
Salinity exceeding 0.5 ppt (parts per thousand) affects drinking water and crop production.
Salinity monitoring is conducted by BWDB with data mapped in EPSG:32646.

## 27. Hakaluki Haor
Hakaluki Haor in Moulvibazar and Sylhet districts is Bangladesh's largest haor, covering
about 181 km² in the dry season and over 238 km² during flood peak. It is a critical
fish habitat and water storage reservoir. It is listed as an Ecologically Critical Area (ECA)
by the Department of Environment. Water depth reaches 4–5 m during peak monsoon.

## 28. Tanguar Haor — Ramsar Wetland
Tanguar Haor in Sunamganj district is a Ramsar Wetland of International Importance,
covering about 100 km². It serves as a breeding and feeding ground for migratory birds
and as a major freshwater fish nursery. Flash floods from upstream hill streams and
illegal fishing threaten the ecosystem. Spatial monitoring uses Sentinel-2 imagery
in EPSG:32646 for seasonal water extent mapping.

## 29. Irrigation Water Use
Agriculture accounts for about 88% of total freshwater withdrawal in Bangladesh.
The Boro rice season (January–April) drives peak irrigation demand, almost entirely
from groundwater. Shallow tube wells (STWs) and deep tube wells (DTWs) pump water
from the alluvial aquifer. About 1.5 million STWs are in operation across the country.

## 30. Water Productivity in Rice
Bangladesh produces over 35 million tonnes of rice annually, making it the world's
third-largest rice producer. However, water productivity (kg rice per m³ of water) is
relatively low in flood-irrigated systems. Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD) irrigation
techniques are promoted to reduce water use per kg of rice by 15–30%.

## 31. Drought-Prone Barind Tract
The Barind Tract is a slightly elevated Pleistocene terrace in Rajshahi, Naogaon, and
Chapai Nawabganj districts. Annual rainfall is 1,400–1,600 mm but highly seasonal.
The tract is drought-prone in the dry season. The Barind Multipurpose Development Authority
(BMDA) manages a deep tube well irrigation network covering over 600,000 hectares.

## 32. Water Quality Monitoring
The Department of Environment (DoE) and BWDB conduct surface water quality monitoring
at key river stations. Parameters measured include dissolved oxygen (DO), biochemical
oxygen demand (BOD), pH, turbidity, and heavy metals. Industrial effluent discharge,
particularly from textile and tannery industries, is the main pollution source in
rivers near Dhaka (Buriganga, Turag, Balu rivers).

## 33. Buriganga River Pollution
The Buriganga River, which flows past Dhaka's western edge, is one of the most polluted
rivers in Asia. Industrial effluents, sewage, and solid waste from Dhaka discharge into
the Buriganga. DO levels frequently drop to near zero in the dry season. The High Court of
Bangladesh has issued orders for environmental restoration. Pollution mapping uses
satellite water quality indices and field survey data in EPSG:32646.

## 34. Padma River Erosion
The Padma is one of the most erosion-active rivers in the world. Bank erosion along the
Padma displaces tens of thousands of people annually. Erosion monitoring uses
multi-temporal satellite imagery (Landsat, Sentinel-2) to map annual bank line changes.
Erosion-vulnerable areas are identified in BWDB's River Management Information System (RMIS).

## 35. River Bank Protection Works
BWDB implements bank protection using Concrete-Cement Block (CCB) revetments and
geobag dumping along high-priority erosion sites. Major protection works have been
completed along the Jamuna (protecting Sirajganj town) and the Padma (protecting
Manikganj, Rajbari, Faridpur). Cost-benefit analysis uses GIS to prioritize protection sites.

## 36. Hydraulic Modeling in Bangladesh
The Institute of Water Modelling (IWM) in Dhaka uses MIKE FLOOD and MIKE 11 models
for river hydraulics, flood forecasting, and coastal simulation. Model inputs include
BWDB gauge data, DEM from SRTM/LiDAR, bathymetric surveys, and meteorological data.
All model domains use EPSG:32646 as the standard projected CRS.

## 37. National Water Management Plan (NWMP)
The NWMP (2001) and its revision under the Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 provide long-term
frameworks for integrated water resource management. Key goals: flood management,
irrigation expansion, drought mitigation, coastal protection, and environmental flow
maintenance. Spatial planning layers are maintained in BWDB's GIS infrastructure.

## 38. Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100
The Bangladesh Delta Plan (BDP 2100), approved in 2018, is a long-term adaptive delta
management strategy. It addresses flood and storm surge risk, water and food security,
and ecosystem conservation under climate change scenarios. The plan identifies six
hotspots: coastal zone, Haor basin, drought-prone areas, char lands, rivers and estuaries,
and urban areas. GIS is central to hotspot delineation in EPSG:32646.

## 39. Climate Change Impact on Water Resources
Climate change projections for Bangladesh (IPCC AR6) indicate:
- Increased monsoon rainfall intensity but shorter wet season
- Reduced dry-season flows in northern rivers due to glacier retreat in the Himalayas
- Higher frequency of extreme rainfall events and flash floods
- Accelerated sea level rise increasing salinity intrusion and coastal flooding
BWDB and IWM use climate-adjusted hydrological models for long-term planning.

## 40. Himalayan Glacial Melt Impact
The Brahmaputra and Ganges both have headwaters fed by Himalayan glaciers. Current glacier
melt is enhancing dry-season flows (glacial surplus phase), but long-term glacier retreat
will reduce dry-season base flows. Bangladesh is a downstream victim of this upstream
change. The impact window is projected at 2050–2100 under high emission scenarios.

## 41. Storm Surge and Coastal Flooding
Tropical cyclones generate storm surges of 3–9 m that inundate coastal Bangladesh.
Cyclone Sidr (2007) produced a 5 m surge, inundating much of the Barguna and Patuakhali
coast. Inundation extent mapping for storm surge events uses BWDB Digital Elevation
Models (DEM) and storm surge model outputs, projected in EPSG:32646.

## 42. Mangrove Role in Coastal Protection
The Sundarbans mangrove forests provide critical protection against storm surge and coastal
erosion. Studies show storm surge attenuation of 50–80 cm per km of mangrove width.
Remote sensing monitors mangrove cover change; NDVI and NDWI indices derived from
Landsat 8 and Sentinel-2 in EPSG:32646 are standard tools.

## 43. Peat and Wetland Carbon Storage
Bangladesh's haors, wetlands, and peat soils store significant carbon. The Sylhet haor
basin contains peat deposits up to 5 m deep. Wetland drainage for agriculture releases
stored carbon. Wetland mapping for carbon inventories uses SAR (Sentinel-1) and optical
imagery combined, with spatial analysis in EPSG:32646.

## 44. Virtual Water and Water Footprint
Bangladesh exports garments (ready-made garments, RMG) and rice. These products embed
large amounts of "virtual water" used in production. The water footprint of Bangladesh's
RMG sector is dominated by cotton growing in other countries, while domestic rice export
draws heavily on irrigated groundwater. Water footprint analysis is increasingly used
in national water accounting frameworks.

## 45. Water Governance and Policy
Water governance in Bangladesh involves multiple ministries and agencies:
- Ministry of Water Resources (MoWR) — policy
- BWDB — infrastructure and flood management
- BADC — irrigation and groundwater
- DPHE — drinking water and sanitation
- DoE — water quality
- IWM — modeling and research
Coordination gaps between agencies are a recognized governance challenge.

## 46. Joint Rivers Commission (JRC)
The JRC is a bilateral body between Bangladesh and India established in 1972 to address
shared river management. It oversees the Ganges Water Treaty implementation and provides
a forum for negotiating water sharing on other rivers including the Teesta. Data exchange
on river flows and floods is a key function of the JRC.

## 47. Waterway Transport
Bangladesh has about 24,000 km of waterways, of which 6,000 km are navigable year-round
and an additional 3,800 km during monsoon. The Bangladesh Inland Water Transport Authority
(BIWTA) manages the network. River transport is critical for cargo and passenger movement,
especially to isolated char and coastal areas. GIS-based waterway mapping uses EPSG:32646.

## 48. Fish Production from Water Bodies
Bangladesh is the world's fourth-largest freshwater fish producer. Open water fisheries
(rivers, haors, floodplains) and closed water aquaculture contribute about equally.
The southwest coastal zone is the main shrimp aquaculture area. Declining fish production
from open waters is linked to habitat loss, overfishing, and reduced connectivity of
floodplains due to embankments.

## 49. Remote Sensing for Water Resource Mapping
Standard remote sensing tools used in Bangladesh water resource management:
- **Sentinel-1 SAR** — flood extent mapping (cloud-penetrating, all-weather)
- **Sentinel-2 MSI** — water body delineation using NDWI (Green–NIR index)
- **Landsat 8/9** — long-term land cover and surface temperature monitoring
- **MODIS** — daily large-area flood monitoring (250 m resolution)
- **SRTM / ALOS PALSAR DEM** — terrain analysis for flood modeling
All processed and stored in EPSG:32646 for national consistency.

## 50. Water-Related Displacement and Climate Migration
Water insecurity — floods, erosion, cyclones, and salinity — is the leading driver of
internal migration in Bangladesh. An estimated 400,000–500,000 people are displaced
annually by river erosion alone. Dhaka receives the largest share of climate migrants.
The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre (IDMC) and UNHCR track displacement data.
GIS vulnerability maps combining flood hazard, erosion risk, and salinity layers in
EPSG:32646 are used for climate migration risk assessment.
